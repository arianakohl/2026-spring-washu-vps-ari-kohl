Designing With Agency

An Interview with Dillon Corsair

Conducted by Ariana Kohl · February 2026

Context

For this interview, I spoke with Dillon Corsair, a fellow Communication Design student at WashU who is minoring in Computer Science and Human-Computer Interaction. I chose Dillon because he has a wide breadth of experience in visual principles from traditional analog work, to digital illustration, animatics, web development, and programming. I felt he could offer a nuanced perspective on the art of effective visual communication strategy.

In this conversation, we discuss how visual principles operate across analog, digital, and reflexive mediums, what it means to design responsibly, how function shapes form, and how designers might maintain agency in the Age of Generative AI. What emerged from our conversation was not fear of change, but a deeper reflection on taste, responsibility, and the value of human judgement.

Interview

Ari: The reason I chose you for this assignment is because you have such a wide breadth of experience in art from traditional analog work all the way to digital illustration, animatics, web development, and programming. You’ve worked across static and interactive mediums. So I want to start broadly: how would you describe the similarities and differences between analog art, digital illustration, and web development?

Dillon: I would say it's the difference between art as expression and art as communication. Illustration and design exist under communication design — you're solving a problem. You're creating an experience for a viewer or user. In web design, everything should be intuitive. It shouldn’t take effort for the user to find what they need. It should look good, but also be structured so people know exactly where to go. With illustration, you’re still directing the viewer’s eye through considering contrast, composition, color, and hierarchy but it’s less about F-pattern or Z-pattern structure and more about storytelling. A bad illustration is one where someone looks at it and doesn’t know what to take away. All of it is communication. You’re guiding someone through information. Studio art is different. It’s more expression than communication. It’s not solving someone else’s problem. It’s personal exploration. The medium and physical interaction are often more central.

“All of it is communication. You’re guiding someone through information.”

Ari: Do you feel like composing for a screen is fundamentally different than composing for print or static media?

Dillon: Yes, because of context and scale. The size of something, whether it’s paper-sized or wall-sized, changes its impact. With web design, there’s reflexivity. The screen size changes. The browser window changes. You have to design something flexible enough to exist across formats. In illustration, there’s a test: if you remove color and turn it black and white, does it still read? If you reduce it to just two or three values, does it still work? Strong composition is built on fundamentals like shapes, contrast, and value — not detail. That principle transfers to web. But web adds expectations. Users expect navigation at the top. They expect certain patterns. Designers have already built solutions to these problems. Ultimately, iteration is crucial. You can know all the theory, but without experimentation and trial and error, you won’t reach the best solution.

Ari: How do you balance following established principles while still being innovative?

Dillon: Innovation isn’t about ignoring rules. The people who break rules have intimate knowledge of them. That’s what allows them to break them with taste. Picasso painted expertly in a traditional way before he distorted anything. Innovators can articulate how and why they’re subverting something. It’s rarely accidental. Learning the principles doesn’t limit innovation unless you let yourself get stuck there. You need practice and understanding before you can meaningfully push boundaries.

“The people who break the rules have an intimate knowledge of them. That’s what allows them to break them with taste.”

Ari: Is a browser just another kind of canvas?

Dillon: In the sense that it’s a surface where you arrange elements, yes. But it has more constraints, as well as more freedom. A beautifully designed website can absolutely display artistry. Craft shows in how it handles reflexivity, polish, animation, consistency with brand. Solving that complexity elegantly is artistic.

Ari: When you’re designing in HTML/CSS, do you feel like a visual designer or like you’re architecting a system?

Dillon: Function comes first. If a website looks beautiful but doesn’t work well, it’s bad design. There’s a design principle: form follows function. What something looks like must support what it does. The elegance often comes from solving the functional problem well. That applies to static design too, like a poster. A beautiful movie poster that doesn’t clearly communicate time, place, or title fails. Beauty matters — psychologically, we prefer beautiful things — but it cannot compromise function. Between two equally functional products, the more beautiful one wins. But if it doesn’t work, beauty doesn’t save it.

“If a website looks beautiful but doesn’t work well, it’s bad design. The elegance often comes from how well you solve the functional problem.”

Ari: What’s your take on AI’s integration into design, coding, and image generation?

Dillon: AI opens doors. It expands what’s possible. But how you use it matters. If you use AI to generate an entire website with no understanding of what’s happening under the hood, you lose agency. You also lose the opportunity to fail, and failure is how you develop taste and judgment. When you struggle through code or illustration and solve problems yourself, you build intuition. My concern with AI is that it trades short-term efficiency for long-term skill development. Using AI as a tutor is different from outsourcing all thinking to it. In image generation, visual artists aren’t as worried as people assume because taste and direction still matter. AI outputs often lack refinement. Without human judgment, the results aren’t as strong.

“When you struggle through something and solve it yourself, you build taste. AI trades short-term efficiency for long-term skill development.”

Ari: So are you not worried at all about AI replacing designers?

Dillon: Well, I guess yes and no. In some cases, AI replaces work that wouldn’t have hired a designer anyway, like with student presentations. That doesn’t concern me much. But for serious organizations that value legitimacy, they still need designers. AI lacks nuance and taste. That said, AI may weaken designers’ bargaining power. Companies now have leverage. Even if AI isn’t as good, it’s “good enough” for many consumers. If something is 60–70% effective, people may accept it. So quality may decline, but companies may prioritize efficiency. It will probably negatively impact opportunities and pay in some ways. Even if it doesn’t fully replace designers, it shifts leverage toward corporations.

“Even if AI isn’t as good, it shifts leverage toward corporations.”

Ari: Are you optimistic about entering this field?

Dillon: No one knows what will happen. When photography was invented, painters feared extinction. Art changed — but it didn’t disappear. AI will change the landscape. But it may even trigger a renaissance in analog aesthetics. We’re already seeing reactions against hyper-polished digital styles in animation, like with Disney and Pixar. Many people value and crave animations with that hand-drawn, textural quality. It's that uniqueness pushing back against uniformity. That's the sort of stuff I like. I’m too deep into this field to quit. I’ll adapt. I’ll learn what I need to. I’m not throwing in the towel because AI made something impressive. Right now, I’m focused on building my own skills. Later, I might experiment more with AI, especially for repetitive or technical tasks, but I want human judgment to remain central.

“No one knows what will happen. Art will change — but it won’t disappear.”

Ari: Any closing thoughts you want to leave us with?

Dillon: I hope people maintain high standards for visual culture. Consumers shape the market. If people reward companies that empower artists and reject low-quality AI output, that influences what gets made. Visual culture depends on public response. I’d like people to appreciate thoughtful design and hold companies accountable for what they produce.
